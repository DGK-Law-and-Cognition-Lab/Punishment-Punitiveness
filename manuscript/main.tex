\leftheader{Simon, Melnikoff, \& Kamper}
\shorttitle{The Dark Side of Punishment}

\abstract{Criminal punishment is publicly justified by its promotion of desirable societal goals: restoring justice, reducing crime, reinforcing societal norms, and rehabilitating offenders. People reliably reach for these prosocial reasons when explaining their own punitive choices. Yet these lofty goals are hard to reconcile with the massive and racialized incarceration regimen in the United States, raising the question of whether prosocial motivations are the primary drivers of criminal punishment. In Study~1, we measured how eighteen psychological constructs relate to punitiveness in 496 U.S. adults. Punitiveness emerged as a dense mindset built largely of dark motivations: hostile aggression measures (hatred, revenge, degradation, endorsement of suffering) correlated far more strongly with punitiveness ($r = .59$) than did legitimate crime concerns ($r = .32$). In Study~2, we applied natural language processing to the open-ended sentencing justifications participants wrote at the end of Study~1. Across methods, 71--96\% of justifications were classified as prosocial, but of 33 text-by-psychology correlations that reached both significance and a meaningful effect size, 32 were negative. The most punitive participants did not endorse more revenge or suffering in their language; they quietly dropped one prosocial theme---rehabilitation---while invoking deterrence, incapacitation, and proportional justice at rates indistinguishable from low-hostile participants. We call this pattern a cultural default with selective suppression: a shared rhetorical script used across the political spectrum. These findings call for a frank acknowledgment that criminal punishment serves also to satisfy the punishing public's own---often unsavory---psychic needs.}

\keywords{punitiveness, criminal punishment, prosocial facade, hostile aggression, natural language processing, sentencing, mass incarceration}
