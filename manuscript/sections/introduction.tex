% === GENERAL INTRODUCTION ===

Criminal punishment has always been in tension with liberal democratic values and enlightened human sensibilities \citep{Allen1999}. As Jeremy Bentham (1789) reminds us: ``all punishment is mischief: all punishment in itself is evil'' (ch.~13, para.~2). For centuries, this tension has precipitated the moral-philosophical endeavor of couching punishment as a just exercise of violence by the state \citep{Allen1999, Tonry2011}. Classically, the well-honed \textit{justifications of punishment} consist of retribution, deterrence, incapacitation, norm expression, and rehabilitation \citep[see][]{Norrie2014, Tonry2011}. These justifications have come to dominate our legal and political discourse. They serve as the gateway to legal education \citep{Ristroph2006}, and they figure prominently in courtroom rhetoric, judicial opinions, legislative debates, and political campaigning. We rely on them also when we boast about getting offenders off the street, locking them up, and sending them a message.

This paper does not attempt to examine the philosophical soundness of these justifications, nor do we explore whether they fit into a coherent moral scheme. Rather, we focus on the core tenet that they are said to serve. At their core, the justifications claim to pursue desirable societal goals: restoring justice, reducing crime, reinforcing social norms, and rehabilitating people who have broken the law---goals that are quintessentially \textit{prosocial}. With few exceptions \citep[e.g.,][]{Stephen1883}, philosophers and legal scholars writing in this field have gone to great lengths to distance criminal punishment from any possible dark motivations. Punishment is said to be meted out based on reasoned policy, impersonally, unemotionally, devoid of revenge, and with respect for the dignity of the punished person \citep[see][see also \citealp{Allen1999}]{Bentham1789, Kant1797, Rawls1955}.

While grounded in moral thinking \citep{Allen1999, Tonry2011}, the prosocial tenet enjoys empirical backing. Experimental economists and evolutionary psychologists highlight the altruistic nature of punishment. Punishment is said to have enabled our predecessors to contain important collective problems such as free-loading, mate poaching, and acts of violence. Punishing such behaviors is said to have enabled non-kin cooperation and promoted societal coexistence \citep{BowlesGintis2011, PriceCosmidesTooby2002, ShinadaYamagishi2007, FehrFischbacher2003, FehrFischbacher2004, FehrGachter2000, FehrGachter2002}.

It must not be overlooked that we are conducting this debate in the shadow of a fifty-year punitive regime that is excessive in its scope \citep{NRC2014, Garland2001}, harshness \citep{Gottschalk2016, JSimon2016}, and disparate racial impact \citep{Armour2020, Alexander2010, Western2006}. The questions that animate this exploration are how we get from the avowed prosociality of punishment to mass incarceration, and why we maintain this regime while knowing that it causes more harm than good \citep[see][]{NRC2014, Tonry2018}. To examine these questions, we go backstage to explore whether the motivations that underlie criminal punishment are entirely well-intended.

\subsection{The Punitive Mindset}

Punitiveness is shaped, in part, by people's perceptions and fears about crime. Belief in high crime rates has been found to be associated with greater punitiveness \citep{ApplegateEtAl2002, Hough2005, Pickett2019}, even as such beliefs are frequently inflated relative to actual crime trends \citep{Pfaff2017, QuillianPager2010}. Fear of becoming a victim also correlates with punitive attitudes \citep{Hough2005}, though this relationship is more modest than is commonly supposed \citep{Gerber2021, Pickett2019}. These crime-related concerns represent the most straightforward prosocial correlates of punitiveness---people who perceive greater threats might reasonably desire stronger responses.

But the literature has also revealed a number of constructs that would not be considered appropriate or prosocial. Punitiveness has been found to be related to emotions---particularly anger \citep{GaultSabini2000, MischkowskiGlocknerLewisch2018}, vengeance \citep{Dewall2007, Jackson2019}, and the urge for retaliation \citep{FergusonRule1983}. Likewise, punitiveness is associated with harsh treatment of offenders, tolerance of prison violence, and the desire to inflict suffering beyond any utilitarian purpose \citep{FincherTetlock2015, GillCerce2021b}. Punitiveness is closely related also to ideology and personality, such as authoritarianism \citep{Altemeyer1981, GerberJackson2013}, affinity to hierarchical social ordering \citep{Pratto1994, SidaniusPratto2001}, and racially resentful dispositions \citep{ChiricosWelchGertz2004, UnneverCullen2010b}.

Unfortunately, this literature is dispersed across a large number of studies, each covering limited slivers of the problem space \citep[for partial exceptions, see][]{KingMaruna2009, GerberJackson2013, Okimoto2012}. The field lacks a macro-level view capable of gauging the relative strength of the various correlates and examining their interrelationships. Another problem plaguing the field is that there is no standard operationalization of punitiveness itself \citep[see][]{Adriaenssen2015, Gerber2021}, with some studies basing it on penological attitudes \citep[e.g.,][]{Cullen1985}, others soliciting sentencing recommendations \citep[e.g.,][]{Carlsmith2008}, and yet others tapping people's support for specific sentencing policies \citep[e.g.,][]{TylerBoeckmann1997}.

\subsection{The Problem of Self-Report}

The notion that punitiveness embodies dark motivations is naturally in tension with the fact that people routinely evoke prosocial justifications to explain their positions on punishment. When asked \textit{why} they endorse harsh sentences, people tend to invoke deterrence, public safety, and incapacitation \citep{Carlsmith2008, Roberts2003}. Yet experimental research demonstrates that these stated justifications do not necessarily match actual punishment behavior: people endorse deterrence and incapacitation in principle but punish according to retributive severity in practice \citep{Carlsmith2006, CarlsmithDarley2008, AharoniFridlund2012, Twardawski2022}. This discrepancy raises a critical question: when people explain their punishment preferences in their own words, are they expressing their genuine motivations, are they being disingenuous, or are they drawing on a culturally available script?

\subsection{The Present Research}

The present research addresses these limitations across two studies. Study~1 conducts a comprehensive correlational investigation of the punitiveness ecosystem, measuring how eighteen psychological constructs relate to punitiveness and to one another. These constructs are organized into four thematic clusters---concern over crime, emotional reactions, hostile aggression, and personality/ideological factors. This design enables us to examine the field at two different magnifications: a detailed construct-level view and a macro-level cluster view. To address the longstanding operationalization problem, we measure punitiveness using a multi-method composite that combines penological attitudes, policy preferences, and a sentencing decision.

Study~2 extends the correlational approach by examining how our participants explain the sentencing decision they made in Study~1. In this vein, we collected open-ended sentencing justifications and subjected them to a multi-method natural language processing (NLP) pipeline. We applied four independent computational text analysis methods---dictionary-based coding, zero-shot classification, forced-choice classification, and Sentence-BERT semantic similarity---to characterize these justifications. This approach allows us to address the prosocial facade hypothesis directly: we can test whether participants' spontaneous language aligns with the prosocial justifications or with the darker psychological factors identified in Study~1.

We preregistered the following primary hypotheses (\url{https://osf.io/kr7y2/}). For Study~1: \textbf{H1}, punitiveness would be positively correlated with all correlate measures; \textbf{H2}, the hostile aggression cluster would show stronger correlations with punitiveness than the crime concerns cluster; \textbf{H3}, most correlate measures would be positively intercorrelated, suggesting a cohesive ``punitiveness mindset.'' For Study~2: \textbf{H4}, participants would predominantly use prosocial language in their sentencing justifications; \textbf{H5}, the semantic content of justifications would be more closely aligned with revenge and infliction of suffering themes than with deterrence and rehabilitation, despite surface-level prosocial framing.
